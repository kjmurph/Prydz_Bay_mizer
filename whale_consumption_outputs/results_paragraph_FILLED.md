# Draft Results Paragraph: Baleen Whale Consumption Comparison

## Context for use

This draft paragraph contextualises the Prydz Bay mizer model results against Savoca et al. (2024). It is designed to:

1. Acknowledge the absolute value uncertainty while emphasising robust relative patterns
2. Highlight the unique contribution of the counterfactual (fishing vs climate-only) analysis
3. Frame Prydz Bay as a regional case study within the broader Southern Ocean context

---

## Draft Results Text

### Comparison with Southern Ocean-wide estimates

The Prydz Bay mizer model produces baleen whale consumption estimates that can be contextualised against the broader Southern Ocean synthesis of Savoca et al. (2024). Direct comparison of absolute consumption values between these approaches is not straightforward: Savoca et al. derive consumption estimates by combining per-capita feeding rates from bio-logging studies with population estimates, whereas our approach emerges from dynamic predator-prey interactions within a size-structured ecosystem model. Additionally, the substantial influence of the ensemble summary statistic (median vs mean) on absolute consumption values in our model reflects the inherent uncertainty in parameterising historical ecosystem states.

Despite these methodological differences, the *relative* magnitude of decline in baleen whale prey consumption shows broad concordance between approaches. Savoca et al. (2024) estimate that current baleen whale krill consumption in CCAMLR Subareas 48.1–48.4 represents approximately 35% of pre-whaling levels (18 Mt yr⁻¹ from an estimated 51 Mt yr⁻¹). In our Prydz Bay model, modern (2001–2010) Antarctic krill consumption by baleen whales under the fishing scenario was **35.0%** of the pre-whaling baseline (1841–1880) using median ensemble values, and **29.7%** using mean values. The corresponding decline in total lower trophic level (LTL) prey consumption was **35.5%** (median) and **38.8%** (mean). This proportional concordance suggests that the relative impacts of whaling on baleen whale consumption may be broadly consistent across Southern Ocean regions, even where absolute consumption rates differ due to regional ecosystem characteristics.

### Novel contribution: Fishing vs climate counterfactual

Our modelling approach enables a comparison that Savoca et al. (2024) explicitly identify as a knowledge gap: disentangling the effects of fishing from climate variability on ecosystem state. By comparing the fishing scenario against a climate-only (unfished) counterfactual, we quantify the additional impact of krill fishing on baleen whale prey consumption beyond what would occur under climate forcing alone.

In the modern period, baleen whale krill consumption under the fishing scenario was **36.8%** of consumption in the climate-only scenario, representing a fishing-attributable deficit of approximately **5,635** tonnes yr⁻¹. For total LTL consumption, the fishing scenario represented **41.3%** of the climate-only scenario, with a deficit of **284,891** tonnes yr⁻¹. This counterfactual comparison reveals that fishing pressure has had a substantial additional impact beyond climate-driven changes. The trajectory of this fishing impact ratio across historical periods shows that the divergence between fishing and climate-only scenarios began during peak whaling (1930-1965, 66.6% of climate-only for LTL) and has intensified through the krill fishing era (1977-1995, 42.7%) to the modern period (41.3%).

The trajectory of this fishing impact ratio across historical periods provides insight into the cumulative effects of sequential resource exploitation in the Prydz Bay ecosystem.

### Diet composition and model behaviour

Antarctic krill represented **1.3% to 2.5%** (median values) of total LTL consumption across all periods in the fishing scenario, indicating relatively stable diet composition through the modelled period. In the climate-only scenario, krill comprised **1.7% to 1.8%** of LTL consumption, showing even greater stability. This relative consistency suggests that prey-switching dynamics in the model do not fundamentally alter the proportional contribution of krill to baleen whale diets, supporting the use of krill consumption as a proxy for total prey demand when comparing with krill-focused estimates such as Savoca et al. (2024). However, it is notable that mean values show higher krill proportions (44-71%) with greater variability, reflecting the influence of parameter uncertainty and extreme ensemble members on mean-based summaries.

## Draft Discussion Text

### Methodological considerations

The comparison between our Prydz Bay mizer model and Savoca et al. (2024) illustrates both the value and limitations of multi-model approaches to understanding historical ecosystem change. Savoca et al. (2024) explicitly characterise their estimates as "back-of-the-envelope calculations" and acknowledge substantial uncertainty, presenting consumption as point estimates with wide ranges (e.g., 51 Mt yr⁻¹, range: 28–90 Mt yr⁻¹). Our size-spectrum approach captures emergent predator-prey dynamics but introduces different sources of uncertainty, particularly in parameterising historical whale populations and their feeding behaviour.

The sensitivity of our absolute consumption estimates to the choice of ensemble summary statistic (median vs mean) reflects the challenge of characterising central tendency in distributions that may be skewed by parameter uncertainty. For example, median krill consumption by baleen whales shows a remarkably close match to Savoca et al.'s relative decline (35.0% vs 35.3%), while mean values show greater divergence (29.7%) due to the influence of extreme parameter combinations in the ensemble. However, the relative consistency in *proportional* change between approaches—particularly when using median values—suggests that the trajectory of ecosystem change, if not its absolute magnitude, is robust across methodological frameworks.

### Regional vs circumpolar context

Prydz Bay (within CCAMLR Division 58.4.2) differs from the Southwest Atlantic sector (Subareas 48.1–48.4) that dominates the Savoca et al. (2024) analysis in several key respects: krill fishing intensity, whale population recovery trajectories, and climate forcing patterns. The comparison of relative consumption decline between these regions may therefore inform whether historical whaling had regionally consistent proportional impacts, or whether local ecosystem characteristics mediated the magnitude of change. Our finding that the median relative decline (35.0% for krill, 35.5% for LTL) closely matches the Savoca et al. estimate (35.3%) despite these regional differences suggests that whaling may have had broadly similar proportional impacts across Southern Ocean regions.

Savoca et al. (2024) note that Division 58.4.2 has an estimated carrying capacity of approximately 61,000 blue whales, which they estimate may have consumed 26 Mt krill yr⁻¹ (range: 15–44 Mt). Our multi-species approach, which includes fin and humpback whales alongside blue whales and captures prey-switching dynamics, provides complementary regional estimates that can inform future assessments of whether current krill biomass can support whale population recovery. The climate-only scenario in our model suggests that in the absence of fishing pressure, whale populations could achieve **85.9%** (LTL) to **95.3%** (krill) of pre-whaling consumption levels under current climate conditions, indicating that ecosystem productivity has not declined to the extent that whale populations have.
